Annotation conventions. These are adapted, in our own words, from a published code-book for
annotating technical concepts in textbook sections; the examples below are from networking.
1. Concepts are nouns or noun phrases. "Retransmitting lost frames" is an activity, not a
   concept; "retransmission" and "frame" are.
2. An abbreviation of a concept is also a concept ("TCP" as well as "Transmission Control
   Protocol"), but an abbreviation given in brackets right after the full name is not extracted
   again as a separate concept.
3. Take the whole noun phrase but drop general modifiers: from "a very long round trip" take
   "round trip".
4. If two noun phrases are each concepts, their combination is a concept too ("link layer
   protocol" when "link layer" and "protocol" both are).
5. Split conjunctions into separate concepts and restore the shared word: "wired and wireless
   links" gives "wired links" and "wireless links".
6. Annotate every variant of a concept: "point-to-point link", "multiple-access link" and
   "shared link" are all variants of "link".
7. Special phrases from a related field (mathematics, statistics) count when the field treats
   them as technical terms ("Poisson distribution").
8. Keep punctuation that is part of a term ("(source, destination) pair").
9. Well-known, important examples (a named protocol, product or system) are concepts.
For scale: in careful annotation about a third of the concepts are single words and about half
are two-word phrases; anything from one to four words is normal.
