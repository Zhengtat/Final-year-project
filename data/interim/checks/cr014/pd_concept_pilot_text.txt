=== SECTION 4.1: Advanced Internetworking > Global Internet ===
[4.1_s0000] At this point, we have seen how to connect a heterogeneous collection of networks to create an internetwork and how to use the simple hierarchy of the IP address to make routing in an internet somewhat scalable.
[4.1_s0001] We say “somewhat” scalable because, even though each router does not need to know about all the hosts connected to the internet, it does, in the model described so far, need to know about all the networks connected to the internet.
[4.1_s0002] Today’s Internet has hundreds of thousands of networks connected to it (or more, depending on how you count).
[4.1_s0003] Routing protocols such as those we have just discussed do not scale to those kinds of numbers.
[4.1_s0004] This section looks at a variety of techniques that greatly improve scalability and that have enabled the Internet to grow as far as it has.
[4.1_s0005] Before getting to these techniques, we need to have a general picture in our heads of what the global Internet looks like.
[4.1_s0006] It is not just a random interconnection of Ethernets, but instead it takes on a shape that reflects the fact that it interconnects many different organizations.
[4.1_s0007] Figure gives a simple depiction of the state of the Internet in 1990.
[4.1_s0008] Since that time, the Internet’s topology has grown much more complex than this figure suggests—we present a slightly more accurate picture of the current Internet in a later section—but this picture will do for now.
[4.1_s0009] One of the salient features of this topology is that it consists of end-user sites (e.g., Stanford University) that connect to service provider networks (e.g., BARRNET was a provider network that served sites in the San Francisco Bay Area).
[4.1_s0010] In 1990, many providers served a limited geographic region and were thus known as regional networks.
[4.1_s0011] The regional networks were, in turn, connected by a nationwide backbone.
[4.1_s0012] In 1990, this backbone was funded by the National Science Foundation (NSF) and was therefore called the NSFNET backbone.
[4.1_s0013] NSFNET gave way to Internet2, which still runs a backbone on behalf of Research and Education institutions in the US (there are similar R&E networks in other countries), but of course most people get their Internet connectivity from commercial providers.
[4.1_s0014] Although the detail is not shown in the figure, today the largest provider networks (they are called tier-1) are typically built from dozens of high-end routers located in major metropolitan areas (colloquially referred to as “NFL cities”) connected by point-to-point links (often with 100 Gbps capacity).
[4.1_s0015] Similarly, each end-user site is typically not a single network but instead consists of multiple physical networks connected by switches and routers.
[4.1_s0016] Notice that each provider and end-user is likely to be an administratively independent entity.
[4.1_s0017] This has some significant consequences on routing.
[4.1_s0018] For example, it is quite likely that different providers will have different ideas about the best routing protocol to use within their networks and on how metrics should be assigned to links in their network.
[4.1_s0019] Because of this independence, each provider’s network is usually a single autonomous system (AS).
[4.1_s0020] We will define this term more precisely in a later section, but for now it is adequate to think of an AS as a network that is administered independently of other ASes.
[4.1_s0021] The fact that the Internet has a discernible structure can be used to our advantage as we tackle the problem of scalability.
[4.1_s0022] In fact, we need to deal with two related scaling issues.
[4.1_s0023] The first is the scalability of routing.
[4.1_s0024] We need to find ways to minimize the number of network numbers that get carried around in routing protocols and stored in the routing tables of routers.
[4.1_s0025] The second is address utilization—that is, making sure that the IP address space does not get consumed too quickly.
[4.1_s0026] Throughout this book, we see the principle of hierarchy used again and again to improve scalability.
[4.1_s0027] We saw in the previous chapter how the hierarchical structure of IP addresses, especially with the flexibility provided by Classless Interdomain Routing (CIDR) and subnetting, can improve the scalability of routing.
[4.1_s0028] In the next two sections, we’ll see further uses of hierarchy (and its partner, aggregation) to provide greater scalability, first in a single domain and then between domains.
[4.1_s0029] Our final subsection looks at IP version 6, the invention of which was largely the result of scalability concerns.
[4.1_s0030] 4.1.1 Routing Areas  As a first example of using hierarchy to scale up the routing system, we’ll examine how link-state routing protocols (such as OSPF and IS-IS) can be used to partition a routing domain into subdomains called areas.
[4.1_s0031] (The terminology varies somewhat among protocols—we use the OSPF terminology here.)
[4.1_s0032] By adding this extra level of hierarchy, we enable single domains to grow larger without overburdening the routing protocols or resorting to the more complex interdomain routing protocols described later.
[4.1_s0033] An area is a set of routers that are administratively configured to exchange link-state information with each other.
[4.1_s0034] There is one special area—the backbone area, also known as area 0.
[4.1_s0035] An example of a routing domain divided into areas is shown in Figure .
[4.1_s0036] Routers R1, R2, and R3 are members of the backbone area.
[4.1_s0037] They are also members of at least one nonbackbone area; R1 is actually a member of both area 1 and area 2.
[4.1_s0038] A router that is a member of both the backbone area and a nonbackbone area is an area border router (ABR).
[4.1_s0039] Note that these are distinct from the routers that are at the edge of an AS, which are referred to as AS border routers for clarity.
[4.1_s0040] Routing within a single area is exactly as described in the previous chapter.
[4.1_s0041] All the routers in the area send link-state advertisements to each other and thus develop a complete, consistent map of the area.
[4.1_s0042] However, the link-state advertisements of routers that are not area border routers do not leave the area in which they originated.
[4.1_s0043] This has the effect of making the flooding and route calculation processes considerably more scalable.
[4.1_s0044] For example, router R4 in area 3 will never see a link-state advertisement from router R8 in area 1.
[4.1_s0045] As a consequence, it will know nothing about the detailed topology of areas other than its own.
[4.1_s0046] How, then, does a router in one area determine the right next hop for a packet destined to a network in another area?
[4.1_s0047] The answer to this becomes clear if we imagine the path of a packet that has to travel from one nonbackbone area to another as being split into three parts.
[4.1_s0048] First, it travels from its source network to the backbone area, then it crosses the backbone, then it travels from the backbone to the destination network.
[4.1_s0049] To make this work, the area border routers summarize routing information that they have learned from one area and make it available in their advertisements to other areas.
[4.1_s0050] For example, R1 receives link-state advertisements from all the routers in area 1 and can thus determine the cost of reaching any network in area 1.
[4.1_s0051] When R1 sends link-state advertisements into area 0, it advertises the costs of reaching the networks in area 1 much as if all those networks were directly connected to R1.
[4.1_s0052] This enables all the area 0 routers to learn the cost to reach all networks in area 1.
[4.1_s0053] The area border routers then summarize this information and advertise it into the nonbackbone areas.
[4.1_s0054] Thus, all routers learn how to reach all networks in the domain.
[4.1_s0055] Note that, in the case of area 2, there are two ABRs and that routers in area 2 will thus have to make a choice as to which one they use to reach the backbone.
[4.1_s0056] This is easy enough, since both R1 and R2 will be advertising costs to various networks, so it will become clear which is the better choice as the routers in area 2 run their shortest-path algorithm.
[4.1_s0057] For example, it is pretty clear that R1 is going to be a better choice than R2 for destinations in area 1.
[4.1_s0058] When dividing a domain into areas, the network administrator makes a tradeoff between scalability and optimality of routing.
[4.1_s0059] The use of areas forces all packets traveling from one area to another to go via the backbone area, even if a shorter path might have been available.
[4.1_s0060] For example, even if R4 and R5 were directly connected, packets would not flow between them because they are in different nonbackbone areas.
[4.1_s0061] It turns out that the need for scalability is often more important than the need to use the absolute shortest path.
[4.1_s0062] Finally, we note that there is a trick by which network administrators can more flexibly decide which routers go in area 0.
[4.1_s0063] This trick uses the idea of a virtual link between routers.
[4.1_s0064] Such a virtual link is obtained by configuring a router that is not directly connected to area 0 to exchange backbone routing information with a router that is.
[4.1_s0065] For example, a virtual link could be configured from R8 to R1, thus making R8 part of the backbone.
[4.1_s0066] R8 would now participate in link-state advertisement flooding with the other routers in area 0.
[4.1_s0067] The cost of the virtual link from R8 to R1 is determined by the exchange of routing information that takes place in area 1.
[4.1_s0068] This technique can help to improve the optimality of routing.
[4.1_s0069] 4.1.2 Interdomain Routing (BGP)
[4.1_s0070] At the beginning of this chapter, we introduced the notion that the Internet is organized as autonomous systems, each of which is under the control of a single administrative entity.
[4.1_s0071] A corporation’s complex internal network might be a single AS, as may the national network of any single Internet Service Provider (ISP).
[4.1_s0072] Figure shows a simple network with two autonomous systems.
[4.1_s0073] The basic idea behind autonomous systems is to provide an additional way to hierarchically aggregate routing information in a large internet, thus improving scalability.
[4.1_s0074] We now divide the routing problem into two parts: routing within a single autonomous system and routing between autonomous systems.
[4.1_s0075] Since another name for autonomous systems in the Internet is routing domains, we refer to the two parts of the routing problem as interdomain routing and intradomain routing.
[4.1_s0076] In addition to improving scalability, the AS model decouples the intradomain routing that takes place in one AS from that taking place in another.
[4.1_s0077] Thus, each AS can run whatever intradomain routing protocols it chooses.
[4.1_s0078] It can even use static routes or multiple protocols, if desired.
[4.1_s0079] The interdomain routing problem is then one of having different ASes share reachability information—descriptions of the set of IP addresses that can be reached via a given AS—with each other.
[4.1_s0080] Challenges in Interdomain Routing  Perhaps the most important challenge of interdomain routing today is the need for each AS to determine its own routing policies.
[4.1_s0081] A simple example routing policy implemented at a particular AS might look like this: “Whenever possible, I prefer to send traffic via AS X than via AS Y, but I’ll use AS Y if it is the only path, and I never want to carry traffic from AS X to AS Y or vice versa.”
[4.1_s0082] Such a policy would be typical when I have paid money to both AS X and AS Y to connect my AS to the rest of the Internet, and AS X is my preferred provider of connectivity, with AS Y being the fallback.
[4.1_s0083] Because I view both AS X and AS Y as providers (and presumably I paid them to play this role), I don’t expect to help them out by carrying traffic between them across my network (this is called transit traffic).
[4.1_s0084] The more autonomous systems I connect to, the more complex policies I might have, especially when you consider backbone providers, who may interconnect with dozens of other providers and hundreds of customers and have different economic arrangements (which affect routing policies) with each one.
[4.1_s0085] A key design goal of interdomain routing is that policies like the example above, and much more complex ones, should be supported by the interdomain routing system.
[4.1_s0086] To make the problem harder, I need to be able to implement such a policy without any help from other autonomous systems, and in the face of possible misconfiguration or malicious behavior by other autonomous systems.
[4.1_s0087] Furthermore, there is often a desire to keep the policies private, because the entities that run the autonomous systems—mostly ISPs—are often in competition with each other and don’t want their economic arrangements made public.
[4.1_s0088] There have been two major interdomain routing protocols in the history of the Internet.
[4.1_s0089] The first was the Exterior Gateway Protocol (EGP), which had a number of limitations, perhaps the most severe of which was that it constrained the topology of the Internet rather significantly.
[4.1_s0090] EGP was designed when the Internet had a treelike topology, such as that illustrated in Figure, and did not allow for the topology to become more general.
[4.1_s0091] Note that in this simple treelike structure there is a single backbone, and autonomous systems are connected only as parents and children and not as peers.
[4.1_s0092] The replacement for EGP was the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP), which has iterated through four versions (BGP-4).
[4.1_s0093] BGP is often regarded as one of the more complex parts of the Internet.
[4.1_s0094] We’ll cover some of its high points here.
[4.1_s0095] Unlike its predecessor EGP, BGP makes virtually no assumptions about how autonomous systems are interconnected—they form an arbitrary graph.
[4.1_s0096] This model is clearly general enough to accommodate non-tree-structured internetworks, like the simplified picture of a multi-provider Internet shown in Figure.
[4.1_s0097] (It turns out there is still some sort of structure to the Internet, as we’ll see below, but it’s nothing like as simple as a tree, and BGP makes no assumptions about such structure.)
[4.1_s0098] Unlike the simple tree-structured Internet shown in Figure, or even the fairly simple picture in Figure, today’s Internet consists of a richly interconnected set of networks, mostly operated by private companies (ISPs) rather than governments.
[4.1_s0099] Many Internet Service Providers (ISPs) exist mainly to provide service to “consumers” (i.e., individuals with computers in their homes), while others offer something more like the old backbone service, interconnecting other providers and sometimes larger corporations.
[4.1_s0100] Often, many providers arrange to interconnect with each other at a single peering point.
[4.1_s0101] To get a better sense of how we might manage routing among this complex interconnection of autonomous systems, we can start by defining a few terms.
[4.1_s0102] We define local traffic as traffic that originates at or terminates on nodes within an AS, and transit traffic as traffic that passes through an AS.
[4.1_s0103] We can classify autonomous systems into three broad types:  -  Stub AS—an AS that only carries local traffic.
[4.1_s0104] The small corporation in Figure is an example of a stub AS.  -  Multihomed Stub AS—an AS that has connections to more than one other AS but that refuses to carry transit traffic, such as the large corporation at the top of Figure.  -  Transit AS—an AS that has connections to more than one other AS and that is designed to carry both transit and local traffic, such as the backbone providers in Figure.
[4.1_s0105] Whereas the discussion of routing in the previous chapter focused on finding optimal paths based on minimizing some sort of link metric, the goals of interdomain routing are rather more complex.
[4.1_s0106] First, it is necessary to find some path to the intended destination that is loop free.
[4.1_s0107] Second, paths must be compliant with the policies of the various autonomous systems along the path—and, as we have already seen, those policies might be almost arbitrarily complex.
[4.1_s0108] Thus, while intradomain focuses on a well-defined problem of optimizing the scalar cost of the path, interdomain focuses on finding a non-looping, policy-compliant path—a much more complex optimization problem.
[4.1_s0109] There are additional factors that make interdomain routing hard.
[4.1_s0110] The first is simply a matter of scale.
[4.1_s0111] An Internet backbone router must be able to forward any packet destined anywhere in the Internet.
[4.1_s0112] That means having a routing table that will provide a match for any valid IP address.
[4.1_s0113] While CIDR has helped to control the number of distinct prefixes that are carried in the Internet’s backbone routing, there is inevitably a lot of routing information to pass around—the number of prefixes has exceeded one million by 2025.
[4.1_s0114] A further challenge in interdomain routing arises from the autonomous nature of the domains.
[4.1_s0115] Note that each domain may run its own interior routing protocols and use any scheme it chooses to assign metrics to paths.
[4.1_s0116] This means that it is impossible to calculate meaningful path costs for a path that crosses multiple autonomous systems.
[4.1_s0117] A cost of 1000 across one provider might imply a great path, but it might mean an unacceptably bad one from another provider.
[4.1_s0118] As a result, interdomain routing advertises only reachability.
[4.1_s0119] The concept of reachability is basically a statement that “you can reach this network through this AS.”
[4.1_s0120] This means that for interdomain routing to pick an optimal path is essentially impossible.
[4.1_s0121] The autonomous nature of interdomain raises issue of trust.
[4.1_s0122] Provider A might be unwilling to believe certain advertisements from provider B for fear that provider B will advertise erroneous routing information.
[4.1_s0123] For example, trusting provider B when he advertises a great route to anywhere in the Internet can be a disastrous choice if provider B turns out to have made a mistake configuring his routers or to have insufficient capacity to carry the traffic.
[4.1_s0124] The issue of trust is also related to the need to support complex policies as noted above.
[4.1_s0125] For example, I might be willing to trust a particular provider only when he advertises reachability to certain prefixes, and thus I would have a policy that says, “Use AS X to reach only prefixes p and q, if and only if AS X advertises reachability to those prefixes.”  Basics of BGP  Each AS has one or more border routers through which packets enter and leave the AS.
[4.1_s0126] In our simple example in Figure, routers R2 and R4 would be border routers.
[4.1_s0127] (Over the years, routers have sometimes also been known as gateways, hence the names of the protocols BGP and EGP).
[4.1_s0128] A border router is simply an IP router that is charged with the task of forwarding packets between autonomous systems.
[4.1_s0129] Each AS that participates in BGP must also have at least one BGP speaker, a router that “speaks” BGP to other BGP speakers in other autonomous systems.
[4.1_s0130] It is common to find that border routers are also BGP speakers, but that does not have to be the case.
[4.1_s0131] BGP does not belong to either of the two main classes of routing protocols, distance-vector or link-state.
[4.1_s0132] Unlike these protocols, BGP advertises complete paths as an enumerated list of autonomous systems to reach a particular network.
[4.1_s0133] It is sometimes called a path-vector protocol for this reason.
[4.1_s0134] The advertisement of complete paths is necessary to enable the sorts of policy decisions described above to be made in accordance with the wishes of a particular AS.
[4.1_s0135] It also enables routing loops to be readily detected.
[4.1_s0136] To see how this works, consider the very simple example network in Figure.
[4.1_s0137] Assume that the providers are transit networks, while the customer networks are stubs.
[4.1_s0138] A BGP speaker for the AS of provider A (AS 2) would be able to advertise reachability information for each of the network numbers assigned to customers P and Q.
[4.1_s0139] Thus, it would say, in effect, “The networks 128.96, 192.4.153, 192.4.32, and 192.4.3 can be reached directly from AS 2.”
[4.1_s0140] The backbone network, on receiving this advertisement, can advertise, “The networks 128.96, 192.4.153, 192.4.32, and 192.4.3 can be reached along the path (AS 1, AS 2).”
[4.1_s0141] Similarly, it could advertise, “The networks 192.12.69, 192.4.54, and 192.4.23 can be reached along the path (AS 1, AS 3).”
[4.1_s0142] An important job of BGP is to prevent the establishment of looping paths.
[4.1_s0143] For example, consider the network illustrated in Figure.
[4.1_s0144] It differs from Figure only in the addition of an extra link between AS 2 and AS 3, but the effect now is that the graph of autonomous systems has a loop in it.
[4.1_s0145] Suppose AS 1 learns that it can reach network 128.96 through AS 2, so it advertises this fact to AS 3, who in turn advertises it back to AS 2.
[4.1_s0146] In the absence of any loop prevention mechanism, AS 2 could now decide that AS 3 was the preferred route for packets destined for 128.96.
[4.1_s0147] If AS 2 starts sending packets addressed to 128.96 to AS 3, AS 3 would send them to AS 1; AS 1 would send them back to AS 2; and they would loop forever.
[4.1_s0148] This is prevented by carrying the complete AS path in the routing messages.
[4.1_s0149] In this case, the advertisement for a path to 128.96 received by AS 2 from AS 3 would contain an AS path of (AS 3, AS 1, AS 2, AS 4).
[4.1_s0150] AS 2 sees itself in this path, and thus concludes that this is not a useful path for it to use.
[4.1_s0151] In order for this loop prevention technique to work, the AS numbers carried in BGP clearly need to be unique.
[4.1_s0152] For example, AS 2 can only recognize itself in the AS path in the above example if no other AS identifies itself in the same way.
[4.1_s0153] AS numbers are now 32-bits long, and they are assigned by a central authority to assure uniqueness.
[4.1_s0154] A given AS will only advertise routes that it considers good enough for itself.
[4.1_s0155] That is, if a BGP speaker has a choice of several different routes to a destination, it will choose the best one according to its own local policies, and then that will be the route it advertises.
[4.1_s0156] Furthermore, a BGP speaker is under no obligation to advertise any route to a destination, even if it has one.
[4.1_s0157] This is how an AS can implement a policy of not providing transit—by refusing to advertise routes to prefixes that are not contained within that AS, even if it knows how to reach them.
[4.1_s0158] Given that links fail and policies change, BGP speakers need to be able to cancel previously advertised paths.
[4.1_s0159] This is done with a form of negative advertisement known as a withdrawn route.
[4.1_s0160] Both positive and negative reachability information are carried in a BGP update message, the format of which is shown in Figure.
[4.1_s0161] (Note that the fields in this figure are multiples of 16 bits, unlike other packet formats in this chapter.)
[4.1_s0162] Unlike the routing protocols described in the previous chapter, BGP is defined to run on top of TCP, the reliable transport protocol.
[4.1_s0163] Because BGP speakers can count on TCP to be reliable, this means that any information that has been sent from one speaker to another does not need to be sent again.
[4.1_s0164] Thus, as long as nothing has changed, a BGP speaker can simply send an occasional keepalive message that says, in effect, “I’m still here and nothing has changed.”
[4.1_s0165] If that router were to crash or become disconnected from its peer, it would stop sending the keepalives, and the other routers that had learned routes from it would assume that those routes were no longer valid.
[4.1_s0166] Common AS Relationships and Policies  Having said that policies may be arbitrarily complex, there turn out to be a few common ones, reflecting common relationships between autonomous systems.
[4.1_s0167] The most common relationships are illustrated in Figure.
[4.1_s0168] The three common relationships and the policies that go with them are as follows:  -  Provider-Customer—\ Providers are in the business of connecting their customers to the rest of the Internet.
[4.1_s0169] A customer might be a corporation, or it might be a smaller ISP (which may have customers of its own).
[4.1_s0170] So the common policy is to advertise all the routes I know about to my customer, and advertise routes I learn from my customer to everyone.  -  Customer-Provider—\
[4.1_s0171] In the other direction, the customer wants to get traffic directed to him (and his customers, if he has them) by his provider, and he wants to be able to send traffic to the rest of the Internet through his provider.
[4.1_s0172] So the common policy in this case is to advertise my own prefixes and routes learned from my customers to my provider, advertise routes learned from my provider to my customers, but don’t advertise routes learned from one provider to another provider.
[4.1_s0173] That last part is to make sure the customer doesn’t find himself in the business of carrying traffic from one provider to another, which isn’t in his interests if he is paying the providers to carry traffic for him.
[4.1_s0174] -  Peer—\ The third option is a symmetrical peering between autonomous systems.
[4.1_s0175] Two providers who view themselves as equals usually peer so that they can get access to each other’s customers without having to pay another provider.
[4.1_s0176] The typical policy here is to advertise routes learned from my customers to my peer, advertise routes learned from my peer to my customers, but don’t advertise routes from my peer to any provider or vice versa.
[4.1_s0177] One thing to note about this figure is the way it has brought back some structure to the apparently unstructured Internet.
[4.1_s0178] At the bottom of the hierarchy we have the stub networks that are customers of one or more providers, and as we move up the hierarchy we see providers who have other providers as their customers.
[4.1_s0179] At the top, we have providers who have customers and peers but are not customers of anyone.
[4.1_s0180] These providers are known as the Tier-1 providers.
[4.1_s0181] Integrating Interdomain and Intradomain Routing  While the preceding discussion illustrates how a BGP speaker learns interdomain routing information, the question still remains as to how all the other routers in a domain get this information.
[4.1_s0182] There are several ways this problem can be addressed.
[4.1_s0183] Let’s start with a very simple situation, which is also very common.
[4.1_s0184] In the case of a stub AS that only connects to other autonomous systems at a single point, the border router is clearly the only choice for all routes that are outside the AS.
[4.1_s0185] Such a router can inject a default route into the intradomain routing protocol.
[4.1_s0186] In effect, this is a statement that any network that has not been explicitly advertised in the intradomain protocol is reachable through the border router.
[4.1_s0187] Recall from the discussion of IP forwarding in the previous chapter that the default entry in the forwarding table comes after all the more specific entries, and it matches anything that failed to match a specific entry.
[4.1_s0188] The next step up in complexity is to have the border routers inject specific routes they have learned from outside the AS.
[4.1_s0189] Consider, for example, the border router of a provider AS that connects to a customer AS.
[4.1_s0190] That router could learn that the network prefix 192.4.54/24 is located inside the customer AS, either through BGP or because the information is configured into the border router.
[4.1_s0191] It could inject a route to that prefix into the routing protocol running inside the provider AS.
[4.1_s0192] This would be an advertisement of the sort, “I have a link to 192.4.54/24 of cost X.”
[4.1_s0193] This would cause other routers in the provider AS to learn that this border router is the place to send packets destined for that prefix.
[4.1_s0194] The final level of complexity comes in backbone networks, which learn so much routing information from BGP that it becomes too costly to inject it into the intradomain protocol.
[4.1_s0195] For example, if a border router wants to inject 10,000 prefixes that it learned about from another AS, it will have to send very big link-state packets to the other routers in that AS, and their shortest-path calculations are going to become very complex.
[4.1_s0196] For this reason, the routers in a backbone network use a variant of BGP called interior BGP (iBGP) to effectively redistribute the information that is learned by the BGP speakers at the edges of the AS to all the other routers in the AS.
[4.1_s0197] (The other variant of BGP, discussed above, runs between autonomous systems and is called exterior BGP, or eBGP).
[4.1_s0198] iBGP enables any router in the AS to learn the best border router to use when sending a packet to any address.
[4.1_s0199] At the same time, each router in the AS keeps track of how to get to each border router using a conventional intradomain protocol with no injected information.
[4.1_s0200] By combining these two sets of information, each router in the AS is able to determine the appropriate next hop for all prefixes.
[4.1_s0201] To see how this all works, consider the simple example network, representing a single AS, in Figure.
[4.1_s0202] The three border routers, A, D, and E, speak eBGP to other autonomous systems and learn how to reach various prefixes.
[4.1_s0203] These three border routers communicate with each other and with the interior routers B and C by building a mesh of iBGP sessions among all the routers in the AS.
[4.1_s0204] Let’s now focus in on how router B builds up its complete view of how to forward packets to any prefix.
[4.1_s0205] Look at the top left of Figure, which shows the information that router B learns from its iBGP sessions.
[4.1_s0206] It learns that some prefixes are best reached via router A, some via D, and some via E. At the same time, all the routers in the AS are also running some intradomain routing protocol such as Routing Information Protocol (RIP) or Open Shortest Path First (OSPF).
[4.1_s0207] (A generic term for intradomain protocols is an interior gateway protocol, or IGP.)
[4.1_s0208] From this completely separate protocol, B learns how to reach other nodes inside the domain, as shown in the top right table.
[4.1_s0209] For example, to reach router E, B needs to send packets toward router C. Finally, in the bottom table, B puts the whole picture together, combining the information about external prefixes learned from iBGP with the information about interior routes to the border routers learned from the IGP.
[4.1_s0210] Thus, if a prefix like 18.0/16 is reachable via border router E, and the best interior path to E is via C, then it follows that any packet destined for 18.0/16 should be forwarded toward C. In this way, any router in the AS can build up a complete routing table for any prefix that is reachable via some border router of the AS.
=== SECTION 9.2: Applications > Multimedia Applications ===
[9.2_s0000] Just like the traditional applications described in the previous section, multimedia applications such as telephony and videoconferencing need their own protocols.
[9.2_s0001] Much of the initial experience in designing protocols for multimedia applications came from the MBone tools—applications such as vat and vic that were developed for use on the MBone, an overlay network that supports IP multicast to enable multiparty conferencing.
[9.2_s0002] (More on overlay networks including the MBone in the next section.)
[9.2_s0003] Initially, each application implemented its own protocol (or protocols), but it became apparent that many multimedia applications have common requirements.
[9.2_s0004] This ultimately led to the development of a number of general-purpose protocols for use by multimedia applications.
[9.2_s0005] We have already seen a number of protocols that multimedia applications use.
[9.2_s0006] The Real-Time Transport Protocol (RTP) provides many of the functions that are common to multimedia applications such as conveying timing information and identifying the coding schemes and media types of an application.
[9.2_s0007] The Resource Reservation Protocol (RSVP) can be used to request the allocation of resources in the network so that the desired quality of service (QoS) can be provided to an application.
[9.2_s0008] We will see how resource allocation interacts with other aspects of multimedia applications later in this section.
[9.2_s0009] In addition to these protocols for multimedia transport and resource allocation, many multimedia applications also need a signalling or session control protocol.
[9.2_s0010] For example, suppose that we wanted to be able to make telephone calls across the Internet (Voice over IP, or VoIP).
[9.2_s0011] We would need some mechanism to notify the intended recipient of such a call that we wanted to talk to her, such as by sending a message to some multimedia device that would cause it to make a ringing sound.
[9.2_s0012] We would also like to be able to support features like call forwarding, three-way calling, etc.
[9.2_s0013] The Session Initiation Protocol (SIP) and H.323 are examples of protocols that address the issues of session control; we begin our discussion of multimedia applications by examining these protocols.
[9.2_s0014] 9.2.1 Session Control and Call Control (SDP, SIP, H.323)
[9.2_s0015] To understand some of the issues of session control, consider the following problem.
[9.2_s0016] Suppose you want to hold a videoconference at a certain time and make it available to a wide number of participants.
[9.2_s0017] Perhaps you have decided to encode the video stream using the MPEG-2 standard, to use the multicast IP address 224.1.1.1 for transmission of the data, and to send it using RTP over UDP port number 4000.
[9.2_s0018] How would you make all that information available to the intended participants?
[9.2_s0019] One way would be to put all that information in an email and send it out, but ideally there should be a standard format and protocol for disseminating this sort of information.
[9.2_s0020] The IETF has defined protocols for just this purpose.
[9.2_s0021] The protocols that have been defined include  -  Session Description Protocol (SDP)  -  Session Announcement Protocol (SAP)  -  Session Initiation Protocol (SIP)  -  Simple Conference Control Protocol (SCCP)
[9.2_s0022] You might think that this is a lot of protocols for a seemingly simple task, but there are many aspects of the problem and several different situations in which it must be addressed.
[9.2_s0023] For example, there is a difference between announcing the fact that a certain conference session is going to be made available on the MBone (which would be done using SDP and SAP) and trying to make an Internet phone call to a certain user at a particular time (which could be done using SDP and SIP).
[9.2_s0024] In the former case, you could consider your job done once you have sent all the session information in a standard format to a well-known multicast address.
[9.2_s0025] In the latter, you would need to locate one or more users, get a message to them announcing your desire to talk (analogous to ringing their phone), and perhaps negotiate a suitable audio encoding among all parties.
[9.2_s0026] We will look first at SDP, which is common to many applications, then at SIP, which is widely used for a number of interactive applications such as Internet telephony.
[9.2_s0027] Session Description Protocol (SDP)  The Session Description Protocol (SDP) is a rather general protocol that can be used in a variety of situations and is typically used in conjunction with one or more other protocols (e.g., SIP).
[9.2_s0028] It conveys the following information:  -  The name and purpose of the session  -  Start and end times for the session  -  The media types (e.g., audio, video) that comprise the session  -  Detailed information required to receive the session (e.g., the multicast address to which data will be sent, the transport protocol to be used, the port numbers, the encoding scheme)  SDP provides this information formatted in ASCII using a sequence of lines of text, each of the form <type>=<value>.
[9.2_s0029] An example of an SDP message will illustrate the main points.
[9.2_s0030] Note that SDP, like HTML, is fairly easy for a human to read but has strict formatting rules that make it possible for machines to interpret the data unambiguously.
[9.2_s0031] For example, the SDP specification defines all the possible information types that are allowed to appear, the order in which they must appear, and the format and reserved words for every type that is defined.
[9.2_s0032] The first thing to notice is that each information type is identified by a single character.
[9.2_s0033] For example, the line tells us that “version” has the value zero; that is, this message is formatted according to version zero of SDP.
[9.2_s0034] The next line provides the “origin” of the session which contains enough information to uniquely identify the session.
[9.2_s0035] larry is a username of the session creator, and 128.112.136.10 is the IP address of his computer.
[9.2_s0036] The number following larry is a session identifier that is chosen to be unique to that machine.
[9.2_s0037] This is followed by a “version” number for the SDP announcement; if the session information was updated by a later message, the version number would be increased.
[9.2_s0038] The next three lines (i, s and u) provide the session name, a session description, and a session Uniform Resource Identifier (URI, as described earlier in this chapter)—information that would be helpful to a user in deciding whether to participate in this session.
[9.2_s0039] Such information could be displayed in the user interface of a session directory tool that shows current and upcoming events that have been advertised using SDP.
[9.2_s0040] The next line (e=...) contains an email address of a person to contact regarding the session.
[9.2_s0041] Figure shows a screen shot of a (now archaic) session directory tool called sdr along with the descriptions of several sessions that had been announced at the time the picture was taken.
[9.2_s0042] Next we get to the technical details that would enable an application program to participate in the session.
[9.2_s0043] The line beginning c=... provides the IP multicast address to which data for this session will be sent; a user would need to join this multicast group to receive the session.
[9.2_s0044] Next we see the start and end times for the session (encoded as integers according to the Network Time Protocol).
[9.2_s0045] Finally, we get to the information about the media for this session.
[9.2_s0046] This session has three media types available—audio, video, and a shared whiteboard application known as wb.
[9.2_s0047] For each media type there is one line of information formatted as follows:  The media types are self-explanatory, and the port numbers in each case are UDP ports.
[9.2_s0048] When we look at the “transport” field, we can see that the wb application runs directly over UDP, while the audio and video are transported using “RTP/AVP”.
[9.2_s0049] This means that they run over RTP and use the application profile known as AVP.
[9.2_s0050] That application profile defines a number of different encoding schemes for audio and video; we can see in this case that the audio is using encoding 0 (which is an encoding using an 8-kHz sampling rate and 8 bits per sample) and the video is using encoding 31, which represents the H.261 encoding scheme.
[9.2_s0051] These “magic numbers” for the encoding schemes are defined in the RFC that defines the AVP profile; it is also possible to describe nonstandard coding schemes in SDP.
[9.2_s0052] Finally, we see a description of the “wb” media type.
[9.2_s0053] All the encoding information for this data is specific to the wb application, and so it is sufficient just to provide the name of the application in the “format” field.
[9.2_s0054] This is analogous to putting application/wb in a MIME message.
[9.2_s0055] Now that we know how to describe sessions, we can look at how they can be initiated.
[9.2_s0056] One way in which SDP is used is to announce multimedia conferences, by sending SDP messages to a well-known multicast address.
[9.2_s0057] The session directory tool shown in Figure would function by joining that multicast group and displaying information that it gleans from received SDP messages.
[9.2_s0058] SDP is also used in the delivery of entertainment video of IP (often called IPTV) to provide information about the video content on each TV channel.
[9.2_s0059] SDP also plays an important role in conjunction with the Session Initiation Protocol (SIP).
[9.2_s0060] With the widespread adoption of Voice over IP (i.e., the support of telephony-like applications over IP networks) and IP-based video conferencing, SIP is now one of the more important members of the Internet protocol suite.
[9.2_s0061] SIP  SIP is an application layer protocol that bears a certain resemblance to HTTP, being based on a similar request/response model.
[9.2_s0062] However, it is designed with rather different sorts of applications in mind and thus provides quite different capabilities than HTTP.
[9.2_s0063] The capabilities provided by SIP can be grouped into five categories:  -  User location—Determining the correct device with which to communicate to reach a particular user  -  User availability—Determining if the user is willing or able to take part in a particular communication session  -  User capabilities—Determining such items as the choice of media and coding scheme to use  -  Session setup—Establishing session parameters such as port numbers to be used by the communicating parties  -  Session management—A range of functions including transferring sessions (e.g., to implement “call forwarding”) and modifying session parameters  Most of these functions are easy enough to understand, but the issue of location bears some further discussion.
[9.2_s0064] One important difference between SIP and, say, HTTP, is that SIP is primarily used for human-to-human communication.
[9.2_s0065] Thus, it is important to be able to locate individual users, not just machines.
[9.2_s0066] And, unlike email, it’s not good enough just to locate a server that the user will be checking on at some later date and dump the message there—we need to know where the user is right now if we want to be able to communicate with him in real time.
[9.2_s0067] This is further complicated by the fact that a user might choose to communicate using a range of different devices, such as using his desktop PC when he’s in the office and using a handheld device when traveling.
[9.2_s0068] Multiple devices might be active at the same time and might have widely different capabilities (e.g., an alphanumeric pager and a PC-based video “phone”).
[9.2_s0069] Ideally, it should be possible for other users to be able to locate and communicate with the appropriate device at any time.
[9.2_s0070] Furthermore, the user must be able to have control over when, where, and from whom he receives calls.
[9.2_s0071] To enable a user to exercise the appropriate level of control over his calls, SIP introduces the notion of a proxy.
[9.2_s0072] A SIP proxy can be thought of as a point of contact for a user to which initial requests for communication with him are sent.
[9.2_s0073] Proxies also perform functions on behalf of callers.
[9.2_s0074] We can see how proxies work best through an example.
[9.2_s0075] Consider the two users in Figure.
[9.2_s0076] The first thing to notice is that each user has a name in the format user@domain, very much like an email address.
[9.2_s0077] When user Bruce wants to initiate a session with Larry, he sends his initial SIP message to the local proxy for his domain, cisco.com.
[9.2_s0078] Among other things, this initial message contains a SIP URI—these are a form of uniform resource identifier which look like this:  A SIP URI provides complete identification of a user, but (unlike a URL) does not provide his location, since that may change over time.
[9.2_s0079] We will see shortly how the location of a user can be determined.
[9.2_s0080] Upon receiving the initial message from Bruce, the proxy looks at the SIP URI and deduces that this message should be sent to the proxy.
[9.2_s0081] For now, we assume that the proxy has access to some database that enables it to obtain a mapping from the name to the IP address of one or more devices at which Larry currently wishes to receive messages.
[9.2_s0082] The proxy can therefore forward the message on to Larry’s chosen device(s).
[9.2_s0083] Sending the message to more than one device is called forking and may be done either in parallel or in series (e.g., send it to his mobile phone if he doesn’t answer the phone at his desk).
[9.2_s0084] The initial message from Bruce to Larry is likely to be a SIP invite message, which looks something like the following:  The first line identifies the type of function to be performed (invite); the resource on which to perform it, the called party (sip:larry@princeton.edu ); and the protocol version (2.0).
[9.2_s0085] The subsequent header lines probably look somewhat familiar because of their resemblance to the header lines in an email message.
[9.2_s0086] SIP defines a large number of header fields, only some of which we describe here.
[9.2_s0087] Note that the Via: header in this example identifies the device from which this message originated.
[9.2_s0088] The Content-Type: and Content-Length: headers describe the contents of the message following the header, just as in a MIME-encoded email message.
[9.2_s0089] In this case, the content is an SDP message.
[9.2_s0090] That message would describe such things as the type of media (audio, video, etc.) that Bruce would like to exchange with Larry and other properties of the session such as codec types that he supports.
[9.2_s0091] Note that the field in SIP provides the capability to use any protocol for this purpose, although SDP is the most common.
[9.2_s0092] Returning to the example, when the invite message arrives at the proxy, not only does the proxy forward the message on toward princeton.edu, but it also responds to the sender of the invite.
[9.2_s0093] Just as in HTTP, all responses have a response code, and the organization of codes is similar to that for HTTP.
[9.2_s0094] In Figure we can see a sequence of SIP messages and responses.
[9.2_s0095] The first response message in this figure is the provisional response 100 trying, which indicates that the message was received without error by the caller’s proxy.
[9.2_s0096] Once the invite is delivered to Larry’s phone, it alerts Larry and responds with a 180 ringing message.
[9.2_s0097] The arrival of this message at Bruce’s computer is a sign that it can generate a “ringtone”.
[9.2_s0098] Assuming Larry is willing and able to communicate with Bruce, he could pick up his phone, causing the message 200 OK to be sent.
[9.2_s0099] Bruce’s computer responds with an ACK, and media (e.g., an RTP-encapsulated audio stream) can now begin to flow between the two parties.
[9.2_s0100] Note that at this point the parties know each others’ addresses, so the ACK can be sent directly, bypassing the proxies.
[9.2_s0101] The proxies are now no longer involved in the call.
[9.2_s0102] Note that the media will therefore typically take a different path through the network than the original signalling messages.
[9.2_s0103] Furthermore, even if one or both of the proxies were to crash at this point, the call could continue on normally.
[9.2_s0104] Finally, when one party wishes to end the session, it sends a BYE message, which elicits a 200 OK response under normal circumstances.
[9.2_s0105] There are a few details that we have glossed over.
[9.2_s0106] One is the negotiation of session characteristics.
[9.2_s0107] Perhaps Bruce would have liked to communicate using both audio and video but Larry’s phone only supports audio.
[9.2_s0108] Thus, Larry’s phone would send an SDP message in its 200 OK describing the properties of the session that will be acceptable to Larry and the device, considering the options that were proposed in Bruce’s invite.
[9.2_s0109] In this way, mutually acceptable session parameters are agreed to before the media flow starts.
[9.2_s0110] The other big issue we have glossed over is that of locating the correct device for Larry.
[9.2_s0111] First, Bruce’s computer had to send its invite to the cisco.com proxy.
[9.2_s0112] This could have been a configured piece of information in the computer, or it could have been learned by DHCP.
[9.2_s0113] Then the cisco.com proxy had to find the princeton.edu proxy.
[9.2_s0114] This could be done using a special sort of DNS lookup that would return the IP address of the SIP proxy for the domain.
[9.2_s0115] (We’ll discuss how DNS can do this in the next section.)
[9.2_s0116] Finally, the princeton.edu proxy had to find a device on which Larry could be contacted.
[9.2_s0117] Typically, a proxy server has access to a location database that can be populated in several ways.
[9.2_s0118] Manual configuration is one option, but a more flexible option is to use the registration capabilities of SIP.
[9.2_s0119] A user can register with a location service by sending a SIP register message to the “registrar” for his domain.
[9.2_s0120] This message creates a binding between an “address of record” and a “contact address”.
[9.2_s0121] An “address of record” is likely to be a SIP URI that is the well-known address for the user (e.g., sip:larry@princeton.edu) and the “contact address” will be the address at which the user can currently be found (e.g., sip:larry@llp-ph.cs.princeton.edu).
[9.2_s0122] This is exactly the binding that was needed by the proxy princeton.edu in our example.
[9.2_s0123] Note that a user may register at several locations and that multiple users may register at a single device.
[9.2_s0124] For example, one can imagine a group of people walking into a conference room that is equipped with an IP phone and all of them registering on it so that they can receive calls on that phone.
[9.2_s0125] SIP is a very rich and flexible protocol that can support a wide range of complex calling scenarios as well as applications that have little or nothing to do with telephony.
[9.2_s0126] For example, SIP supports operations that enable a call to be routed to a “music-on-hold” server or a voicemail server.
[9.2_s0127] It is also easy to see how it could be used for applications like instant messaging, and standardization of SIP extensions for such purposes is ongoing.
[9.2_s0128] H.323  The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) has also been very active in the call control area, which is not surprising given its relevance to telephony, the traditional realm of that body.
[9.2_s0129] Fortunately, there has been considerable coordination between the IETF and the ITU in this instance, so that the various protocols are somewhat interoperable.
[9.2_s0130] The major ITU recommendation for multimedia communication over packet networks is known as H.323, which ties together many other recommendations, including H.225 for call control.
[9.2_s0131] The full set of recommendations covered by H.323 runs to many hundreds of pages, and the protocol is known for its complexity, so it is only possible to give a brief overview of it here.
[9.2_s0132] H.323 is popular as a protocol for Internet telephony, including video calls, and we consider that class of application here.
[9.2_s0133] A device that originates or terminates calls is known as an H.323 terminal; this might be a workstation running an Internet telephony application, or it might be a specially designed “appliance”—a telephone-like device with networking software and an Ethernet port, for example.
[9.2_s0134] H.323 terminals can talk to each other directly, but the calls are frequently mediated by a device known as a gatekeeper.
[9.2_s0135] Gatekeepers perform a number of functions such as translating among the various address formats used for phone calls and controlling how many calls can be placed at a given time to limit the bandwidth used by the H.323 applications.
[9.2_s0136] H.323 also includes the concept of a gateway, which connects the H.323 network to other types of networks.
[9.2_s0137] The most common use of a gateway is to connect an H.323 network to the public switched telephone network (PSTN) as illustrated in Figure.
[9.2_s0138] This enables a user running an H.323 application on a computer to talk to a person using a conventional phone on the public telephone network.
[9.2_s0139] One useful function performed by the gatekeeper is to help a terminal find a gateway, perhaps choosing among several options to find one that is relatively close to the ultimate destination of the call.
[9.2_s0140] This is clearly useful in a world where conventional phones greatly outnumber PC-based phones.
[9.2_s0141] When an H.323 terminal makes a call to an endpoint that is a conventional phone, the gateway becomes the effective endpoint for the H.323 call and is responsible for performing the appropriate translation of both signalling information and the media stream that need to be carried over the telephone network.
[9.2_s0142] An important part of H.323 is the H.245 protocol, which is used to negotiate the properties of the call, somewhat analogously to the use of SDP described above.
[9.2_s0143] H.245 messages might list a number of different audio codec standards that it can support; the far endpoint of the call would reply with a list of its own supported codecs, and the two ends could pick a coding standard that they can both live with.
[9.2_s0144] H.245 can also be used to signal the UDP port numbers that will be used by RTP and Real-Time Control Protocol (RTCP) for the media stream (or streams—a call might include both audio and video, for example) for this call.
[9.2_s0145] Once this is accomplished, the call can proceed, with RTP being used to transport the media streams and RTCP carrying the relevant control information.
[9.2_s0146] 9.2.2 Resource Allocation for Multimedia Applications  As we have just seen, session control protocols like SIP and H.323 can be used to initiate and control communication in multimedia applications, while RTP provides transport-level functions for the data streams of the applications.
[9.2_s0147] A final piece of the puzzle in getting multimedia applications to work is making sure that suitable resources are allocated inside the network to ensure that the quality of service needs of the application are met.
[9.2_s0148] We presented a number of methods for resource allocation in an earlier chapter.
[9.2_s0149] The motivation for developing these technologies was largely for the support of multimedia applications.
[9.2_s0150] So how do applications take advantage of the underlying resource allocation capabilities of the network?
[9.2_s0151] It is worth noting that many multimedia applications run successfully over “best-effort” networks, such as the public Internet.
[9.2_s0152] The wide array of commercial VoIP services (such as Skype) are a testimony to the fact that you only have to worry about resource allocation when resources are not abundant—and in many parts of today’s Internet, resource abundance is the norm.
[9.2_s0153] A protocol like RTCP can help applications in best-effort networks, by giving the application detailed information about the quality of service that is being delivered by the network.
[9.2_s0154] Recall that RTCP carries information about the loss rate and delay characteristics between participants in a multimedia application.
[9.2_s0155] An application can use this information to change its coding scheme—changing to a lower bitrate codec, for example, when bandwidth is scarce.
[9.2_s0156] Note that, while it might be tempting to change to a codec that sends additional, redundant information when loss rates are high, this is frowned upon; it is analogous to increasing the window size of TCP in the presence of loss, the exact opposite of what is required to avoid congestion collapse.
[9.2_s0157] As discussed in an earlier chapter, Differentiated Services (DiffServ) can be used to provide fairly basic and scalable resource allocation to applications.
[9.2_s0158] A multimedia application can set the differentiated services code point (DSCP) in the IP header of the packets that it generates in an effort to ensure that both the media and control packets receive appropriate quality of service.
[9.2_s0159] For example, it is common to mark voice media packets as “EF” (expedited forwarding) to cause them to be placed in a low-latency or priority queue in routers along the path, while the call signalling (e.g., SIP) packets are often marked with some sort of “AF” (assured forwarding) to enable them to be queued separately from best-effort traffic and thus reduce their risk of loss.
[9.2_s0160] Of course, it only makes sense to mark the packets inside the sending host or appliance if network devices such as routers pay attention to the DSCP.
[9.2_s0161] In general, routers in the public Internet ignore the DSCP, providing best-effort service to all packets.
[9.2_s0162] However, enterprise or corporate networks have the ability to use DiffServ for their internal multimedia traffic, and frequently do so.
[9.2_s0163] Also, even residential users of the Internet can often improve the quality of VoIP or other multimedia applications just by using DiffServ on the outbound direction of their Internet connections, as illustrated in Figure.
[9.2_s0164] This is effective because of the asymmetry of many broadband Internet connections: If the outbound link is substantially slower (i.e., more resource constrained) than the inbound, then resource allocation using DiffServ on that link may be enough to make all the difference in quality for latency- and loss-sensitive applications.
[9.2_s0165] While DiffServ is appealing for its simplicity, it is clear that it cannot meet the needs of applications under all conditions.
[9.2_s0166] For example, suppose the upstream bandwidth in Figure is only 100 kbps, and the customer attempts to place two VoIP calls, each with a 64-kbps codec.
[9.2_s0167] Clearly the upstream link is now more than 100% loaded, which will lead to large queuing delays and lost packets.
[9.2_s0168] No amount of clever queuing in the customer’s router can fix that.
[9.2_s0169] The characteristics of many multimedia applications are such that, rather than try to squeeze too many calls into a too-narrow pipe, it would be better to block one call while allowing another to proceed.
[9.2_s0170] That is, it is better to have one person carrying on a conversation successfully while another hears a busy signal than to have both callers experiencing unacceptable audio quality at the same time.
[9.2_s0171] We sometimes refer to such applications as having a steep utility curve, meaning that the utility (usefulness) of the application drops rapidly as the quality of service provided by the network degrades.
[9.2_s0172] Multimedia applications often have this property, whereas many traditional applications do not.
[9.2_s0173] Email, for example, continues to work quite well even if delays run into the hours.
[9.2_s0174] Applications with steep utility curves are often well suited to some form of admission control.
[9.2_s0175] If you cannot be sure that sufficient resources will always be available to support the offered load of the applications, then admission control provides a way to say “no” to some applications while allowing others to get the resources they need.
[9.2_s0176] We saw one way to do admission control using RSVP in an earlier chapter, and we will return to that shortly, but multimedia applications that use session control protocols provide some other admission control options.
[9.2_s0177] The key point to observe here is that session control protocols like SIP or H.323 often involve some sort of message exchange between an endpoint and another entity (SIP proxy or H.323 gatekeeper) at the beginning of a call or session.
[9.2_s0178] This can provide a handy means to say “no” to a new call for which sufficient resources are not available.
[9.2_s0179] As an example, consider the network in Figure.
[9.2_s0180] Suppose the wide area link from the branch office to the head office has enough bandwidth to accommodate three VoIP calls simultaneously using 64-kbps codecs.
[9.2_s0181] Each phone already needs to communicate with the local SIP proxy or H.323 gatekeeper when it begins to place a call, so it is easy enough for the proxy/gatekeeper to send back a message that tells the IP phone to play a busy signal if that link is already fully loaded.
[9.2_s0182] The proxy or gatekeeper can even deal with the possibility that a particular IP phone might be making multiple calls at the same time and that different codec speeds might be used.
[9.2_s0183] However, this scheme will work only if no other device can overload the link without first talking to the gatekeeper or proxy.
[9.2_s0184] DiffServ queuing can be used to ensure that, for example, a PC engaged in a file transfer doesn’t interfere with the VoIP calls.

[9.2_s0191] We refer to the sort of admission control just described as off-path, in the sense that the device making admission control decisions does not sit on the data path where resources need to be allocated.
[9.2_s0192] The obvious alternative is on-path admission control, and the standard example of a protocol that does on-path admission control in IP networks is the Resource Reservation Protocol (RSVP).
[9.2_s0193] We saw in an earlier chapter how RSVP can be used to ensure that sufficient resources are allocated along a path, and it is straightforward to use RSVP in applications like those described in this section.
[9.2_s0194] The one detail that still needs to be filled in is how the admission control protocol interacts with the session control protocol.
[9.2_s0195] Coordinating the actions of an admission control (or resource reservation) protocol and a session control protocol is not rocket science, but it does require some attention to details.
[9.2_s0196] As an example, consider a simple telephone call between two parties.
[9.2_s0197] Before you can make a reservation, you need to know how much bandwidth the call is going to use, which means you need to know what codecs are to be used.
[9.2_s0198] That implies you need to do some of the session control first, to exchange information about the codecs supported by the two phones.
[9.2_s0199] However, you can’t do all the session control first, because you wouldn’t want the phone to ring before the admission control decision had been made, in case admission control failed.
[9.2_s0200] Figure illustrates this situation where SIP is used for session control and RSVP is used to make the admission control decision (successfully in this case).
[9.2_s0201] The main thing to notice here is the interleaving of session control and resource allocation tasks.
[9.2_s0202] Solid lines represent SIP messages, dashed lines represent RSVP messages.
[9.2_s0203] Note that SIP messages are transmitted direction from phone to phone in this example (i.e., we have not shown any SIP proxies), whereas the RSVP messages are also processed by the routers in the middle as the check for sufficient resources to admit the call.
[9.2_s0204] We begin with an initial exchange of codec information in the first two SIP messages (recall that SDP is used to list available codecs, among other things).
[9.2_s0205] PRACK is a “provisional acknowledgment”.
[9.2_s0206] Once these messages have been exchanged, RSVP PATH messages, which contain a description of the amount of resources that will be required, can be sent as the first step in reserving resources in both directions of the call.
[9.2_s0207] Next, RESV messages can be sent back to actually reserve the resources.
[9.2_s0208] Once a RESV is received by the initiating phone, it can send an updated SDP message reporting the fact that resources have been reserved in one direction.
[9.2_s0209] When the called phone has received both that message and the RESV from the other phone, it can start to ring and tell the other phone that resources are now reserved in both directions (with the SDP message) and also notify the calling phone that it is ringing.
[9.2_s0210] From here on, normal SIP signalling and media flow, similar to that shown in Figure, proceeds.
[9.2_s0211] Again we see how building applications requires us to understand the interaction between different building blocks (SIP and RSVP, in this case).
[9.2_s0212] The designers of SIP actually made some changes to the protocol to enable this interleaving of functions between protocols with different jobs, hence our repeated emphasis in this book on focusing on complete systems rather than just looking at one layer or component in isolation from the other parts of the system.
